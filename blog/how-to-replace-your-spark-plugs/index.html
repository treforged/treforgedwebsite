How to Replace Your Spark Plugs: A Step-by-Step Guide

Learn how to replace your own spark plugs with the right tools, gapping, and torque specs, and see how much you'll save versus shop labor rates.

October 5, 2026 · 9 min read · TRE Forged

If your engine has started idling rough, hesitating under acceleration, or burning a little more gas than it used to, worn spark plugs are one of the most common culprits. The good news is that replacing them is one of the more approachable DIY jobs under the hood. With a handful of basic tools, a torque wrench, and about an hour of your Saturday, you can swap out old plugs yourself and pocket the labor fee a shop would normally charge. This guide walks through the tools, the gapping process, the torque specs, and every step in between so you can tackle it with confidence.

Before you start, know that spark plug jobs vary quite a bit by engine layout. A simple four-cylinder with plugs right on top of the engine might take 30 minutes. A V6 or V8 with plugs buried under the intake manifold can take two or three hours and some extra patience. For the exact procedure, torque spec, and gap size for your specific year, make, and model, it's worth pulling up CarCareKiosk, which has free model-specific video walkthroughs that show you exactly where your plugs live and how your engine is laid out.

Why Replace Your Own Spark Plugs

Most shops charge between $120 and $300 for a spark plug replacement, and a big chunk of that is labor, not parts. A set of four to six plugs typically costs $20 to $60 depending on whether you need standard copper, platinum, or iridium plugs. If your shop quotes you two hours of labor at $130 to $180 an hour just to access and swap plugs that take you 45 minutes in your own driveway, you can see where the real savings come from. Doing it yourself once or twice can cover the cost of the tools outright, and after that, every future swap is nearly pure savings.

This job also pairs naturally with other maintenance you're likely already tracking. What You'll Need: Tools and Materials

Spark plug swaps don't require a huge toolbox, but a few specialty items make the job much easier and safer for the threads in your cylinder head.

  • New spark plugs matching your vehicle's specified type and heat range
  • A spark plug socket (usually 5/8 inch or 13/16 inch, with a rubber insert to grip the plug)
  • A ratchet and a 3 to 6 inch extension, sometimes a long extension or universal joint for hard-to-reach plugs
  • A torque wrench rated for inch-pounds or foot-pounds, depending on your spec
  • A gap gauge (wire-style or feeler gauge type) for checking and adjusting the gap
  • Dielectric grease for the boot connections
  • Anti-seize compound (only if your manufacturer recommends it, many modern plugs come pre-coated)
  • A small funnel or magnetic pickup tool in case a plug gets dropped into the engine bay
  • Shop rags and a flashlight or work light

Most of these items are inexpensive and reusable for years, which is part of why this job pays for itself quickly compared to paying a shop every time.

Difficulty, Time, and Cost Estimate

For a straightforward four-cylinder engine with easy plug access, this is a beginner-friendly job rated easy to moderate, taking roughly 30 to 60 minutes. For V6 and V8 engines where plugs sit underneath the intake manifold or require removing other components to reach, the difficulty climbs to moderate, and the time can stretch to two or three hours, especially on your first attempt. Budget $30 to $70 for plugs and, if you don't already own them, another $40 to $80 for a spark plug socket, gap gauge, and torque wrench. Compare that to a $150 to $300 shop bill, and even factoring in tool costs, most DIYers break even on the very first job.

Understanding Gapping and Torque Specs

The gap is the small space between the center electrode and the side electrode where the spark actually jumps. Too narrow a gap and the spark can be weak; too wide and it may misfire under load. Many modern plugs come pre-gapped from the factory, but it's still worth checking with a gap gauge before installation since shipping and handling can nudge them out of spec. Typical gaps range from about 0.028 to 0.060 inches depending on the engine, so always confirm the exact number in your owner's manual or on a sticker under the hood, since using the wrong gap can cause a rough idle or check engine light.

Torque matters just as much as gap. Over-tightening can crack the porcelain insulator or strip the aluminum threads in your cylinder head, which turns a simple job into an expensive repair. Under-tightening can cause poor cylinder sealing and a loss of power. Most spark plugs torque to somewhere between 10 and 20 foot-pounds, though some call for inch-pounds in the 70 to 170 range, so again, verify your specific number. Never rely on just "feel" for this step. Always use a torque wrench. It's the single biggest difference between a job that lasts 60,000 miles and one that leaves you with a stripped head.

Step-by-Step Spark Plug Replacement

  1. Park on a level surface, let the engine cool completely (at least 30 minutes after driving), and disconnect the negative battery terminal for safety.
  2. Locate the ignition coils or spark plug wires on top of the engine and remove any engine covers blocking access.
  3. Label or photograph the wiring so coils or wires go back to the correct cylinder.
  4. Disconnect the coil pack or wire boot from the first spark plug, twisting gently rather than yanking on the wire itself.
  5. Use compressed air or a rag to clear any debris from around the spark plug well so dirt doesn't fall into the cylinder.
  6. Insert the spark plug socket and extension, and carefully loosen the old plug counterclockwise.
  7. Remove the old plug and inspect it; unusual coloring or heavy deposits can hint at other engine issues worth investigating.
  8. Check the gap on your new plug with a gap gauge and adjust gently if needed to match spec.
  9. Apply a light coat of anti-seize if recommended, then thread the new plug in by hand to avoid cross-threading.
  10. Using your torque wrench, tighten to the manufacturer's exact spec, no more and no less.
  11. Apply a dab of dielectric grease inside the boot, then reconnect the coil or wire firmly.
  12. Repeat for each remaining cylinder, working one at a time so you never lose track of wiring.
  13. Reconnect the battery, start the engine, and listen for smooth, even idling.
Safety warning: Always let the engine fully cool before starting this job. Spark plugs and surrounding metal can cause serious burns if the engine has been running recently, and working around a hot exhaust manifold is a common way first-timers get hurt.

When to See a Mechanic Instead

If your engine requires removing the intake manifold, fuel rail, or other major components to reach the plugs, and you're not comfortable with that level of disassembly, it's reasonable to hand the job to a professional. The same goes if you strip a thread, drop a plug into a hard-to-reach spot, or notice oil or coolant near the spark plug wells, since that can signal a deeper issue like a failing valve cover gasket. There's no shame in knowing your limits, and a shop visit here is far cheaper than a cracked cylinder head from a rushed DIY attempt.

FAQ

Common questions

How often should I replace my spark plugs?

It depends on the plug material and your vehicle, but most copper plugs last 20,000 to 30,000 miles, while platinum or iridium plugs often go 60,000 to 100,000 miles. Check your owner's manual for the exact interval.

Can I do this myself if I've never worked on a car before?

Yes, on most four-cylinder engines this is a beginner-friendly job with basic tools. Engines where plugs sit under the intake manifold are tougher, so watch a model-specific video first to gauge the difficulty before you start.

What happens if I use the wrong gap or torque spec?

A gap that's too narrow or wide can cause misfires, rough idling, or a check engine light. Incorrect torque can strip the threads in your cylinder head or crack the plug, so always confirm both numbers for your specific engine.

Do I really need a torque wrench for this job?

Yes. Hand-tightening by feel is one of the most common ways people damage their cylinder head threads or under-seat a plug. A torque wrench is inexpensive and removes the guesswork entirely.
