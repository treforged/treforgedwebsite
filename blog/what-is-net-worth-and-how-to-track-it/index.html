What Is Net Worth and Why You Should Track It in 2026

Learn what net worth really means, how to calculate it step by step, and why tracking it regularly is the single best way to measure your financial progress.

August 7, 2026 · 8 min read · TRE Forged

If someone asked you right now whether you're better off financially than you were a year ago, could you answer with a number? Most people can't, because they're tracking the wrong things. They watch their paycheck, their credit card balance, or their checking account, but none of those tell the full story. Net worth does. It's the one number that captures everything you own and everything you owe, and it's the closest thing personal finance has to a scoreboard.

This guide breaks down exactly what net worth means, how to calculate yours in a few minutes, and why checking it regularly (not obsessively) can change how you make money decisions. No spreadsheets degrees required, just a calculator and some honesty about your numbers.

What Net Worth Actually Means

Net worth is simply the difference between what you own (your assets) and what you owe (your liabilities). The formula is short enough to remember forever: Assets minus Liabilities equals Net Worth. If you owned a $20,000 car outright and had $5,000 in savings, your assets would total $25,000. If you had no debt, your net worth would also be $25,000. Add a $15,000 auto loan and $3,000 in credit card debt, and your net worth drops to $7,000, even though you still have the same car and savings.

This is why net worth feels different from looking at your bank balance. Your checking account might show $2,000 and feel fine, while your credit cards quietly carry $9,000 in debt. Net worth forces both sides of that picture into view at the same time. It doesn't care how good your income looks on paper, only what's left after debts are subtracted from what you actually own.

Why Net Worth Beats Salary as a Financial Scoreboard

Income is easy to obsess over because it's the number on your pay stub, but income alone tells you nothing about your financial health. Someone earning $110,000 a year with $40,000 in student loans, a car loan, and no savings could easily have a lower net worth than someone earning $55,000 who has been saving steadily and has no debt. Salary measures cash flow in a given month. Net worth measures accumulated progress over years.

Net worth also reveals patterns that monthly budgets miss. You might feel like you're doing everything right, paying bills on time, sticking to a budget, and still not see your net worth grow if high-interest debt is eating your progress from the other side. Tracking it regularly connects the dots between your daily spending choices and your long-term financial trajectory, which is exactly the gap that tools like a structured budgeting framework are designed to close.

There's also a psychological benefit. Watching your net worth climb, even slowly, gives you tangible proof that your effort is working. That feedback loop is often what keeps people motivated to keep paying down debt or saving, long after the initial excitement of a New Year's resolution fades.

How to Calculate Your Net Worth Step by Step

Calculating net worth is a math exercise, not a mystery. Here's how to do it in under 20 minutes, even if you've never done it before.

  1. List every asset you own and its current value: checking and savings accounts, retirement accounts, investment accounts, the estimated resale value of your car, and your home's estimated market value.
  2. List every liability you owe and its current balance: credit cards, student loans, auto loans, personal loans, and mortgage balance.
  3. Add up all your assets to get one total number.
  4. Add up all your liabilities to get one total number.
  5. Subtract liabilities from assets. That result is your net worth, and it can absolutely be negative, especially early in adulthood.

For example, imagine Maria has $3,500 in checking and savings, $12,000 in a 401(k), and a car worth $8,000, giving her $23,500 in assets. She owes $6,000 in student loans and $2,500 on a credit card, totaling $8,500 in liabilities. Her net worth is $15,000. That single number tells her more about her financial standing than any pay stub could.

What Counts as an Asset (and What Doesn't)

Assets are anything with real, sellable value. Cash in checking and savings accounts counts fully, since it's already liquid. Retirement accounts like a 401(k) or IRA count at their current balance, even though you can't touch that money without penalties right now. Homes and cars count too, but use realistic current market value, not what you originally paid or what you wish they were worth.

Where people go wrong is inflating their asset list with things that don't hold real financial value. Furniture, clothing, electronics, and collectibles are usually worth far less than people assume once you account for depreciation and resale friction. It's fine to leave these off entirely or estimate them very conservatively. The goal is an honest number, not an impressive one.

  • Include: cash, savings, retirement accounts, investment accounts, home equity value, vehicle resale value
  • Be cautious with: jewelry, collectibles, business equity that's hard to value
  • Usually skip: everyday personal belongings, clothing, furniture

What Counts as a Liability

Liabilities are every debt you're currently on the hook for, listed at their current outstanding balance, not the original loan amount. This includes credit card balances, student loans, auto loans, personal loans, medical debt, and any remaining mortgage balance. If you owe your sibling $500, technically that counts too, even though it feels informal.

A common mistake is leaving out debts that don't come with a monthly statement, like an interest-free loan from a family member or a buy-now-pay-later balance split across four payments. These are real liabilities and belong on the list. Leaving them off doesn't make the debt disappear, it just makes your net worth number less accurate and less useful.

If you're carrying multiple debts and aren't sure which to pay off first, understanding the tradeoffs in debt payoff strategies can help you decide, since the method you choose will directly affect how quickly your liabilities shrink and your net worth climbs.

How Often You Should Track It

Checking net worth too often can backfire, since normal month-to-month fluctuations from market dips or a big bill can feel discouraging even when your long-term trend is positive. Checking it once a month is usually the sweet spot for most people, giving you enough data points to see a trend without obsessing over noise.

Some people prefer a quarterly check-in instead, especially if their financial situation is stable and they're mostly focused on long-term saving and investing. What matters most is consistency. Pick a recurring day, like the first Sunday of the month, and calculate the same way every time so your numbers are comparable over time.

It also helps to keep a simple running log, even a basic spreadsheet with a date and a number, so you can see the trend line rather than a single snapshot. A $15,000 net worth today means very little on its own, but a net worth that moved from $8,000 to $15,000 over eighteen months tells a real story of progress.

Common Net Worth Mistakes to Avoid

The biggest mistake is inconsistency, switching how you value your home or car each time you calculate, which makes trend lines meaningless. Pick a reasonable valuation method (a real estate site's estimate for your home, a trade-in value for your car) and stick with it every single time you update your number.

Another common mistake is panicking over a temporary dip. If your investments drop during a market downturn or you take on a short-term loan for a necessary car repair, your net worth may dip for a month or two. That's normal and doesn't erase months of progress. The goal is the long-term trend, not any single month's result.

Finally, many people avoid calculating net worth altogether because they're afraid the number will be negative or embarrassing. A negative net worth is extremely common, especially for people early in their careers or carrying student loans, and it's simply a starting point, not a verdict on your character or future. If you're just getting your finances organized for the first time, pairing this with building your first budget gives you both the scoreboard and the game plan to move it in the right direction.

Quick Recap

  1. Net worth equals total assets minus total liabilities.
  2. It matters more than salary because it reflects real financial progress, not just cash flow.
  3. List all assets: cash, savings, retirement accounts, investments, home and vehicle value.
  4. List all liabilities: credit cards, loans, mortgage balance, and informal debts.
  5. Subtract liabilities from assets to get your current net worth, even if it's negative.
  6. Track it monthly or quarterly using consistent valuation methods.
  7. Focus on the long-term trend, not single-month dips, and don't fear a negative starting number.
FAQ

Common questions

What is a good net worth to have by age 30?

There's no single universal number, since it depends heavily on income, location, and debt like student loans. A more useful goal than hitting a specific figure is having a positive and steadily growing net worth, even if it's modest, because the trend matters more than the exact amount at any one age.

Is it normal to have a negative net worth?

Yes, especially in your 20s or early 30s if you're carrying student loans, a car loan, or credit card debt. A negative net worth simply means your liabilities currently outweigh your assets, and it's a starting point to track and improve, not a sign of financial failure.

How often should I calculate my net worth?

Once a month is ideal for most people, giving enough time between checks to see meaningful movement without getting distracted by short-term market or spending fluctuations. Some people prefer quarterly check-ins if their finances are stable and mostly focused on long-term saving.

Does net worth include my home and car?

Yes, both count as assets at their current realistic market value, not the original purchase price. If you still owe money on either, the remaining loan balance should be listed separately as a liability, which is subtracted out when calculating your total net worth.
