How to Read Your Check Engine Light with an OBD2 Scanner

Learn how to buy a cheap OBD2 scanner, pull your car's trouble codes, and figure out if it's a DIY fix or worth paying a shop's diagnostic fee.

October 6, 2026 · 8 min read · TRE Forged

That little amber engine icon on your dashboard has a way of ruining your whole day. The good news is that in most cases, your car is already telling you exactly what's wrong, you just need the right $20 tool to translate the message. Every gasoline car sold in the United States since 1996 is required to have an OBD2 (On Board Diagnostics, second generation) port, and plugging a cheap scanner into it takes less time than finding a parking spot at a busy shop. This guide walks you through buying a scanner, pulling the code, understanding what it means, and deciding whether you can fix it yourself or whether it's smarter to pay a mechanic's diagnostic fee.

What You'll Need

You do not need a mechanic's toolbox for this job. The whole point of an OBD2 scanner is that it does the hard diagnostic work for you, so the shopping list is short and inexpensive.

  • A basic OBD2 code reader (entry-level models from Autel, Innova, BlueDriver, or similar run $20 to $100)
  • Alternatively, a Bluetooth OBD2 adapter paired with a free smartphone app (Torque, Car Scanner, or your scanner brand's own app)
  • Your car's owner's manual, to confirm the exact port location
  • A pen and paper or your phone's notes app to write down the code before you clear anything
  • A flashlight, since the OBD2 port is almost always tucked under the dashboard in shadow

If you only plan to do this once or twice, borrowing a scanner is also an option. Many auto parts stores, including AutoZone and Advance Auto Parts, will read your codes for free in their parking lot. That said, owning a $25 reader pays for itself the first time your check engine light comes on again, and it means you're not dependent on store hours.

Difficulty, Time, and Cost

Reading a code is genuinely one of the easiest tasks in this whole hobby. Plugging in the scanner and pulling the code takes about five to ten minutes once you know where the port is. Understanding the code and deciding what to do about it might take another fifteen to thirty minutes of research, especially the first time.

Difficulty: Beginner. Time: 15 to 40 minutes total. Cost: $0 if you use a free parts-store reader, or $20 to $100 if you buy your own scanner, which is worth it for repeat use. Compare that to a shop's diagnostic fee, which typically runs $90 to $150 just to tell you what the code already told you for free.

Step-by-Step: Pulling the Code

  1. Park on level ground, set the parking brake, and turn the ignition off completely.
  2. Locate the OBD2 port. On most cars it's within two feet of the steering wheel, often under the dash on the driver's side, sometimes behind a small plastic flap. Check CarCareKiosk for a free video specific to your exact year, make, and model if you can't find it right away.
  3. Plug the scanner's connector firmly into the port. It only fits one way, so don't force it.
  4. Turn the key to the "on" position without starting the engine, or start the engine if your scanner's instructions specify that. Most basic readers want the ignition in the "on" (accessory) position, not running.
  5. Follow the prompts on the scanner's screen. Many will auto-detect your vehicle's make and year, or ask you to confirm it manually.
  6. Select "Read Codes" from the menu. Within a few seconds you'll see one or more codes, formatted like P0300 or P0442.
  7. Write down every code shown, along with any "pending" or "permanent" codes, before you do anything else.
  8. Do not clear the code yet. Clearing it erases the data a mechanic could use later and can also turn off the light before the underlying issue is actually fixed.
Safety note: Never attempt to read or clear codes while the engine is running and the car is in gear, and keep your hands and the scanner's cable clear of the accelerator pedal and steering column while you work under the dash. If your check engine light is accompanied by a flashing light, a loss of power, overheating, or a burning smell, pull over safely and stop driving the car; a flashing light in particular usually signals an active misfire that can damage your catalytic converter within minutes.

Decoding What the Code Actually Means

An OBD2 code is a five-character shorthand, not a full diagnosis. The first letter tells you the system (P for Powertrain, B for Body, C for Chassis, U for Network), and most check engine codes start with P. The number after that narrows things down, and a quick search of the exact code plus your car's year and model will usually turn up forums, videos, and manufacturer bulletins describing the likely cause.

Some codes are refreshingly simple. P0442 usually points to a small evaporative emissions leak, which is often just a loose or cracked gas cap, something you can fix for free in thirty seconds. Others, like P0300 (random cylinder misfire) or P0171 (system too lean), can stem from several different root causes ranging from a cheap vacuum hose to an expensive fuel injector, so the code is a starting point for further testing rather than a final answer.

It also helps to note whether the light is steady or flashing, and whether the scanner shows the code as "confirmed" or "pending." A pending code means the issue has been detected once but hasn't repeated enough times to trigger the light fully, which sometimes resolves itself. A confirmed code tied to a flashing light is the one that deserves the most urgent attention.

DIY or Shop? How to Decide

Once you know the code, the real decision is whether this is a job for your own driveway or a job for a professional. A handful of codes are famous for being cheap, low-risk fixes: a loose gas cap, a dirty mass airflow sensor that just needs cleaning, a worn oxygen sensor, or a disconnected vacuum hose you can spot and reattach by eye. If the fix involves a part you can see and reach without lifting the car, and the part itself costs under $50, it's usually worth attempting yourself, especially with a model-specific video from CarCareKiosk open on your phone for reference.

On the other hand, codes related to the transmission, the catalytic converter, airbag or ABS systems, or anything requiring specialized tools like a fuel pressure gauge or a smoke machine are better left to a shop. The diagnostic fee you pay there isn't wasted money, it's paying for equipment and experience that confirms the actual failed part rather than guessing and replacing parts one at a time. Keeping a regular maintenance schedule also reduces how often you'll see these lights in the first place, since many codes trace back to skipped service intervals.

When to See a Mechanic Instead

If the code points to something structural, like a transmission code (P07xx series) or anything involving the airbag or brake control modules, stop and book a shop appointment rather than guessing. The same goes for any situation where the light is flashing, where you smell fuel or burning, or where the car is noticeably losing power or overheating. Trying to save a diagnostic fee on a problem you can't safely verify yourself often costs more in the long run if a cheap part fails and damages an expensive one around it.

It's also worth being honest about your own comfort level and tools. If a fix requires lifting the car on a jack stand, disconnecting the battery, or working near the fuel system, and you've never done those things before, a shop visit is the safer and often cheaper option once you factor in your time and the risk of a mistake. Understanding what car maintenance actually costs can help you budget for the repairs you do decide to outsource.

Quick Recap

  1. Buy or borrow a basic OBD2 scanner, since entry-level models cost $20 to $100.
  2. Park, set the brake, and turn off the engine before you start.
  3. Find the OBD2 port, usually under the dash near the steering wheel.
  4. Plug in the scanner and turn the key to the "on" position.
  5. Select "Read Codes" and write down every code shown.
  6. Do not clear the code until you've researched and addressed the cause.
  7. Look up the exact code plus your car's year and model to learn the likely cause.
  8. Check whether the light is steady or flashing, and treat flashing lights as urgent.
  9. Decide if the fix is a simple, visible, cheap part you can tackle yourself.
  10. If the code involves the transmission, emissions system, or safety modules, book a shop visit instead.

FAQ

Common questions

Can I really read my own check engine light without paying a mechanic?

Yes. A basic OBD2 scanner costs $20 to $100 and plugs into a port under your dashboard, letting you pull the same trouble code a shop would read, often for free at an auto parts store.

Will clearing the code make the check engine light go away for good?

Clearing a code turns the light off temporarily, but if the underlying problem isn't fixed, the light will come back once the car's computer detects the issue again, sometimes within a single drive.

Is it safe to keep driving with the check engine light on?

A steady light usually means it's safe to drive carefully for a short time while you diagnose the issue, but a flashing light signals an active problem like a misfire and means you should stop driving as soon as it's safe to do so.

How often should I check my car with an OBD2 scanner?

You don't need to scan proactively on a schedule, but it's worth plugging in any time the check engine light appears, and occasionally checking for pending codes if your car has recently felt off even without the light on.
