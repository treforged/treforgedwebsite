How to Lubricate Door Hinges, Hood, and Trunk Latches (A 5-Minute DIY Fix)

Stop squeaky doors and rust buildup with this quick DIY guide to lubricating car door hinges, hood, and trunk latches the right way.

September 28, 2026 · 7 min read · TRE Forged

That squeak every time you open your driver's door isn't just annoying, it's your car telling you that metal is grinding against metal with nothing to protect it. Left alone long enough, that friction wears down hinge pins, invites rust into the joint, and eventually makes your doors, hood, or trunk feel stiff and reluctant to open. The good news is that fixing it is one of the easiest and cheapest maintenance tasks you can do yourself, no lift, no special tools, and no mechanical experience required. Grab a rag and a can of the right lubricant, and you can knock this out in about five minutes per hinge.

Why This Five-Minute Job Matters

Every hinge and latch on your car, the ones on your doors, hood, and trunk, is a metal-on-metal pivot point that opens and closes thousands of times over the life of the vehicle. Automakers apply grease at the factory, but that grease dries out, washes away in the rain, or gets contaminated with dirt and road grime over the years. Once the original lubricant is gone, you're left with bare metal rubbing against bare metal every single time you get in or out of the car.

That friction does two things. First, it causes the squeaking and creaking that makes an otherwise nice car sound like it's falling apart. Second, and more importantly, it accelerates wear and creates tiny abrasions in the metal where moisture can collect and start rust. Rust in a hinge doesn't just look bad, it can eventually cause the hinge to bind, sag, or fail to latch securely. A door that doesn't close firmly is a safety issue, not just a cosmetic one.

This is exactly the kind of small, preventative task that belongs in a broader maintenance routine. If you don't already have a system for tracking jobs like this, it's worth reading through how to build a car maintenance schedule so tasks like hinge lubrication happen automatically twice a year instead of only when the squeak gets embarrassing.

What You'll Need

This is a genuinely low-cost job. Most of what you need can be bought for under $15 total, and a single can of lubricant will last through several years of hinge maintenance for an entire household of cars.

  • A can of white lithium grease or silicone spray lubricant (avoid plain WD-40 as your primary lubricant, more on that below)
  • A clean microfiber cloth or shop rag
  • A small wire brush or old toothbrush for scrubbing off rust and grime
  • Painter's tape or newspaper to protect paint and carpet from overspray
  • Nitrile or latex gloves, optional but handy for keeping grease off your hands

Estimated time: 15 to 20 minutes for the whole car (about 5 minutes per hinge or latch). Difficulty: Beginner-friendly, no prior experience needed. Estimated cost: $10 to $15 for supplies that will last for years.

Choosing the Right Lubricant

Not all lubricants are created equal, and this is where a lot of first-timers get it wrong. WD-40 is a fantastic penetrating oil and a great first step for breaking loose rusted or stuck hardware, but it's a poor long-term lubricant because it's thin, evaporates quickly, and actually attracts dust and grime over time. If you use WD-40 alone on a hinge, you may find the squeak comes right back within a few weeks.

For hinges that pivot (like your door and hood hinges), white lithium grease is the gold standard. It's thick, sticks to metal well, resists washing away in rain, and holds up in a wide range of temperatures. It comes in both spray cans and tube form, though the spray version is easier to apply precisely into tight hinge gaps.

For latches and strikers, which are the mechanisms that catch and hold the door, hood, or trunk closed, a dry silicone spray lubricant is often the better choice. It won't attract as much dirt as grease and it won't stain clothing or fabric if you brush against it while loading groceries into the trunk. Some people use a light coat of white lithium grease on latches too, and that works fine, just wipe away any excess so it doesn't transfer onto clothes or cargo.

Step-by-Step: Lubricating Hinges and Latches

Work through each door, the hood, and the trunk one at a time so you don't miss any pivot points. Most cars have between 8 and 12 hinges and latches total once you count every door, the hood, and the trunk or hatch.

  1. Open the door, hood, or trunk fully and wipe down the hinge area with a clean rag to remove loose dirt, dust, and old grease buildup.
  2. If you see surface rust or caked-on grime, scrub it gently with a wire brush or old toothbrush until the metal is clean and dry.
  3. Lay down painter's tape or newspaper on the surrounding paint and carpet if you're worried about overspray, especially near door jambs and trunk carpeting.
  4. Spray a light, even coat of white lithium grease directly into the hinge pivot point, working the door or hood open and closed a few times so the lubricant works its way into the joint.
  5. Move to the latch mechanism (the part that clicks shut on the door frame or trunk lip) and apply a light coat of silicone spray or grease to the moving parts of the striker and latch plate.
  6. Wipe away any excess lubricant with your rag so it doesn't drip onto paint, clothing, or cargo.
  7. Repeat for every door, the hood, and the trunk or hatch, then test each one by opening and closing it a few times to confirm the squeak is gone and everything latches securely.

Common Mistakes and Safety Notes

The biggest mistake people make is overspraying. A little lubricant goes a long way, and excess grease just attracts dirt, drips onto paint, or worse, gets tracked onto seats and clothing. Apply a thin, even coat and wipe away anything that isn't absorbed into the joint.

Another common error is skipping the cleaning step. Spraying fresh grease over old, dirty, rusted grease just seals in grit and grime, which can actually make the hinge feel gritty rather than smooth. Always wipe and, if needed, scrub the area clean before applying new lubricant.

Safety warning: Never spray lubricant near the door's electrical connectors, wiring harnesses, or the seatbelt pretensioner mechanisms found in some door frames. Keep lubricant away from rubber weatherstripping too, since some petroleum-based products can cause rubber seals to swell or degrade over time. When in doubt, spray the lubricant onto a rag first and apply it by hand to the exact pivot point instead of spraying directly into the door jamb.

Also be mindful of the hood and trunk while they're propped open. Make sure the support strut or prop rod is securely holding the panel before you lean in close to work on the hinge, since a hood or trunk lid falling unexpectedly can cause a serious injury.

When to See a Mechanic Instead

Lubrication solves squeaks caused by dry or dirty hinges, but it won't fix a hinge that's physically worn out, bent, or a door that sags visibly when opened. If your door drops noticeably at the outer edge when open, if you hear grinding or clunking rather than a simple squeak, or if the latch doesn't catch securely even after cleaning and lubricating it, that points to worn hinge pins, a damaged striker, or a bent latch mechanism that needs professional repair or replacement. A door that won't latch securely is a safety concern and shouldn't be left unresolved.

Quick Recap

  1. Gather white lithium grease, silicone spray, a rag, and a wire brush or old toothbrush.
  2. Open the door, hood, or trunk and wipe down the hinge to remove dirt and grime.
  3. Scrub off any surface rust with a wire brush before applying lubricant.
  4. Protect nearby paint and carpet with painter's tape or newspaper if desired.
  5. Spray white lithium grease into the hinge pivot and work it in by opening and closing a few times.
  6. Apply silicone spray or a light coat of grease to the latch and striker mechanism.
  7. Wipe away excess lubricant so nothing drips or transfers onto clothing or cargo.
  8. Test each door, the hood, and the trunk to confirm the squeak is gone and everything latches firmly.
  9. Repeat this process roughly twice a year, or sooner if squeaks return.

FAQ

Common questions

How often should I lubricate my car's door hinges and latches?

About twice a year is a good baseline for most drivers, ideally once in spring and once in fall. If you live somewhere with heavy rain, snow, or road salt, doing it every three to four months helps prevent rust from taking hold.

Can I use WD-40 instead of white lithium grease?

WD-40 is fine as a first step to loosen rust or grime, but it evaporates quickly and isn't a good long-term lubricant on its own. Follow it up with white lithium grease or a silicone spray for lasting protection.

Will lubricating my hinges stain my clothes or the door panel?

It can if you apply too much or don't wipe away the excess. Use a light, even coat, wipe off any drips immediately, and consider silicone spray near areas where clothing or cargo might brush against the latch.

Is this safe to do myself even if I've never worked on a car before?

Yes, this is one of the most beginner-friendly maintenance tasks there is. Just follow the safety notes about keeping lubricant away from wiring and rubber seals, and make sure any prop rods holding the hood or trunk open are secure before you lean in close.
