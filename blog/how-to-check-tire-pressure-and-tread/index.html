How to Check Your Tire Pressure and Tread (And Why It Saves You Real Money)

Learn to check tire PSI and tread depth in minutes with a simple gauge and the penny test, and see how proper pressure saves fuel and extends tire life.

July 14, 2026 · 8 min read · TRE Forged

Your tires are the only part of your car actually touching the road, yet most drivers never check them until something feels wrong. A five-minute habit, done once a month, can save you money on gas, help your tires last thousands of miles longer, and catch a slow leak before it becomes a flat on the highway. This guide walks you through checking tire pressure and tread depth from scratch, no mechanic required.

What you'll need: a tire pressure gauge (digital or analog, $8 to $20), your owner's manual or the sticker inside your driver's door jamb, a penny (or a $20 bill, or a dedicated tread depth gauge), a notepad or your phone to jot down numbers, and a portable air compressor or access to a gas station air pump if you need to add air. Total cost for a basic gauge and a penny is under $15, and the whole process takes about 10 to 15 minutes for all four tires.

Difficulty: beginner-friendly. Time: 10 to 15 minutes. Cost: $0 to $20 if you already have a gauge, or up to $30 for a compressor.

Why Tire Pressure and Tread Actually Matter

Underinflated tires flex more as they roll, which creates extra friction and heat. That heat wears down the rubber faster and forces your engine to work harder just to push the car forward, which quietly drains your fuel economy. The U.S. Department of Energy estimates that properly inflated tires can improve gas mileage by up to 3 percent, and some studies suggest the number is higher for tires that are significantly low. Over a year of driving, that adds up to real dollars saved at the pump.

Overinflated tires cause a different problem. They ride harder, wear unevenly in the center of the tread, and reduce the contact patch that keeps you gripping the road in rain or snow. Either extreme shortens tire life and increases your risk in an emergency stop or sudden swerve. Tread depth matters just as much because it is what channels water away from under your tire. Bald or balding tires lose that grip fast, especially in wet conditions, and that is when hydroplaning and skidding become real dangers.

What Your Actual PSI Should Be

Ignore the number printed on the tire's sidewall. That number is the maximum pressure the tire can safely hold, not the recommended pressure for your vehicle. Your car's correct PSI is set by the manufacturer and is almost always found on a sticker inside the driver's side door jamb, or in your owner's manual under a section labeled tires or tire pressure. Many everyday sedans and small SUVs call for somewhere between 30 and 35 PSI, but trucks, vans, and vehicles that regularly carry heavy loads may need more, and some vehicles even list a different PSI for the front versus the rear axle.

Exact specs vary by year, make, and model, so do not assume your friend's car uses the same number as yours. If you cannot find the sticker or the manual, a quick search for your specific vehicle usually turns up the spec, or you can check CarCareKiosk, which has free, model-specific how-to videos where you can look up your exact year, make, and model and see exactly where the sticker is located on your car.

Step-by-Step: Checking and Adjusting Your Tire Pressure

Check pressure when your tires are cold, meaning the car has not been driven for at least three hours, or has been driven less than a mile. Heat from driving temporarily raises pressure and gives you a false reading.

  1. Locate your recommended PSI on the door jamb sticker or in the owner's manual.
  2. Remove the valve stem cap from the first tire and set it somewhere you won't lose it.
  3. Press the tire gauge firmly onto the valve stem until the hissing stops and you get a steady reading. If air is escaping loudly around the gauge, reposition it for a tighter seal.
  4. Read the number. If it is below spec, add air in short bursts using a compressor or gas station pump, checking the gauge between bursts so you do not overshoot.
  5. If it is above spec, press the small pin inside the valve stem with the tip of your gauge or a fingernail to release small amounts of air, checking frequently.
  6. Replace the valve cap and repeat for all four tires, plus your spare if it holds air.
  7. Write down the numbers so you can track whether any tire is losing pressure faster than the others, which can signal a slow leak.

If one tire consistently reads several pounds lower than the others week after week, that is a sign of a puncture, a damaged valve stem, or a bead leak, and it's worth having a shop check it before it strands you.

The Penny Test: Checking Tread Depth

Tread depth is even simpler to check than pressure, and you likely already have the tool in your pocket. Take a standard penny and insert it into one of the tire's tread grooves with Lincoln's head facing down toward the tire.

  • If part of Lincoln's head is covered by the tread, you have more than 2/32 of an inch of tread remaining, which is generally considered acceptable.
  • If you can see all of Lincoln's head, your tread is worn down to the legal minimum in most states and it's time to start shopping for new tires.
  • For an earlier warning, some people use a quarter instead. If the tread covers part of Washington's head, you still have a healthy amount of tread left, often around 4/32 of an inch, which is the point many tire experts recommend replacing tires anyway for better wet-weather safety.

Repeat the penny test in at least three spots across each tire (inner edge, center, and outer edge), since uneven wear is common and a single spot check can miss a problem. Uneven wear patterns can also point to alignment issues, so if one edge of a tire is noticeably more worn than the rest, that is worth mentioning to a mechanic.

Reading the Warning Signs Between Checks

Beyond the monthly check, pay attention to how your car feels and sounds day to day. A dashboard tire pressure warning light (TPMS) means at least one tire has dropped meaningfully below spec and should be checked immediately, not next week. Vibration at highway speed, a car that pulls to one side, or a thumping noise can all point to tire problems ranging from low pressure to internal damage.

Safety warning: Never drive on a tire that is visibly flat, bulging, or has a nail or screw embedded in it, even if it still holds air. Pull over safely and call for roadside assistance or change to your spare rather than risk a blowout at speed.

Also check pressure before any long road trip and after any season change, since cold weather alone can drop tire pressure noticeably as the air inside contracts. A tire that was fine in September might read several PSI lower by the first cold morning in November.

When to See a Mechanic Instead

This is a job almost anyone can do themselves with basic tools, but there are times to hand it off to a professional. If you find a nail, screw, or visible puncture, if a tire loses pressure rapidly within a day or two, if you notice a bulge or crack in the sidewall, or if your tread is worn unevenly in a pattern that looks like cupping or scalloping, get a trained technician to look at it. Tire rotation, balancing, and alignment are also best left to a shop with the right equipment, even though checking pressure and tread is squarely a driveway job.

FAQ

Common questions

How often should I check my tire pressure and tread?

Check tire pressure at least once a month and before any long road trip, since pressure naturally drops with colder temperatures and slow leaks. Check tread depth with the penny test roughly every other month, or anytime you notice uneven wear, vibration, or reduced grip in wet conditions.

Can I really check tire pressure and tread myself, or do I need a mechanic?

You can absolutely do both yourself with a basic tire gauge and a penny, no special training needed. It's one of the simplest maintenance tasks in car ownership, though punctures, sidewall damage, or rapid pressure loss should be handled by a professional.

What PSI should my tires be at?

Use the number on the sticker inside your driver's door jamb or in your owner's manual, not the number printed on the tire's sidewall, since that figure is the tire's maximum rating, not the recommended pressure. Most passenger cars fall between 30 and 35 PSI, but it varies by vehicle, so check your specific spec or look up your exact model on CarCareKiosk.

What happens if I drive on a tire that fails the penny test?

Driving on tread that has worn down to the legal minimum significantly increases your stopping distance and risk of hydroplaning in wet weather. It's best to replace tires before they reach that point, ideally when tread still covers part of a quarter's head for extra margin.

How to check tire tread at home

Insert a standard penny into a tread groove with Lincoln's head facing down toward the tire. If part of his head is covered you have more than 2/32 of an inch of tread left, and if you can see all of his head the tire is worn out and needs replacing. Repeat the test at the inner edge, the center and the outer edge of each tire, since uneven wear is common and one spot can miss it.
