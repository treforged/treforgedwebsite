How to Check Your Tire Pressure and Tread (The Right Way)

Learn how to check tire pressure and tread depth at home in under 15 minutes, save on gas and tires, and know when it's time to see a mechanic.

July 14, 2026 · 8 min read · TRE Forged

Your tires are the only part of your car actually touching the road, yet most drivers never check them until something feels wrong. That's a mistake, and an expensive one. Underinflated tires quietly drain your fuel economy, wear out faster, and can fail at highway speed. Bald tread turns a rainy commute into a hydroplaning nightmare. The good news: checking both pressure and tread takes less than 15 minutes, costs almost nothing, and is one of the easiest ways to protect your family and your wallet at the same time.

This guide walks you through exactly how to do it, no mechanic required. As always, exact tire size and recommended pressure vary by year, make, and model, so if you want a video walkthrough for your specific vehicle, CarCareKiosk has free step-by-step videos you can search by plate or VIN.

What You'll Need

You don't need a garage full of tools for this job. A basic tire pressure gauge, a penny (or a tread depth gauge if you want to be extra precise), and a tire chalk or marker if you want to track wear over time are all it takes. Most gauges, whether the dial type, digital type, or simple pen-style stick gauge, cost between $8 and $20 at any auto parts store. If your car has a tire inflation kit or an air compressor at home, that's a bonus but not required since gas stations almost always have coin-operated air pumps.

  • Tire pressure gauge (digital or dial, $8 to $20)
  • A penny for the tread test (free)
  • Tire chalk or a piece of tape (optional, for tracking)
  • Your owner's manual or the sticker inside the driver's door jamb
  • Access to an air source (home compressor or gas station air pump, often $1 to $2)

Estimated time: 10 to 15 minutes for all four tires. Difficulty: Beginner-friendly, no lifting or tools beyond a gauge. Cost: Under $25 total if you're buying a gauge for the first time, and closer to free after that.

Step 1: Find Your Correct PSI

Here's a detail that trips up a lot of new car owners: the number printed on the tire's sidewall is not your recommended pressure. That number is the maximum pressure the tire can safely hold, not what your vehicle manufacturer recommends for ride comfort, handling, and even tire wear. The actual number you want is almost always printed on a sticker inside the driver's side door jamb, or listed in your owner's manual.

Most passenger cars recommend somewhere between 30 and 35 PSI (pounds per square inch), but trucks, SUVs, and vehicles that carry heavier loads can call for higher numbers, sometimes with a different front and rear spec. Some vehicles even list a separate pressure for when you're carrying extra passengers or towing. Write this number down or snap a photo of the door jamb sticker with your phone so you always have it handy.
