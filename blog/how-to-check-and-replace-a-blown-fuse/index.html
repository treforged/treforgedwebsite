How to Check and Replace a Blown Car Fuse (Without Paying a Shop $80 to Do It)

Learn how to find your fuse box, test a suspect fuse with a $10 tool, and swap it safely, plus a real DIY versus shop cost breakdown.

September 23, 2026 · 8 min read · TRE Forged

Your radio suddenly goes silent, your dome light stops working, or your turn signal quits clicking, and the first thought is usually "this is going to cost me." In a huge number of cases, though, the real problem is a five-dollar part you can find and swap yourself in under fifteen minutes: a blown fuse. Learning to check and replace one is one of the cheapest, lowest-risk repairs a first-time DIYer can tackle, and it is a great gateway into more hands-on car care.

This guide walks through how fuses work, how to find your fuse boxes, how to test a suspect fuse with a simple tool, and how to swap it correctly. We will also compare what this job costs you at home versus what a shop typically charges just to diagnose the same issue. As always, exact fuse box locations, amp ratings, and fuse types vary by year, make, and model, so keep your owner's manual handy and consider looking up your specific vehicle on CarCareKiosk, which has free video walkthroughs for thousands of individual car models.

Why Fuses Blow and What the Symptoms Look Like

A fuse is a small sacrificial part designed to fail on purpose. Inside it is a thin metal strip rated for a specific amount of current, measured in amps. If a circuit draws more current than that strip can handle, whether from a short, a worn wire, a failing component, or a surge, the strip melts and breaks the circuit before it can damage more expensive wiring or fry a control module. That is the whole point: a blown fuse is often the car protecting itself, not the car failing.

The symptoms are usually narrow and specific rather than car-wide. You might lose one turn signal but not the other, lose your cigarette lighter or USB charging port, lose your radio and dashboard clock, or find your interior lights suddenly dead while everything else works fine. That narrowness is actually a helpful diagnostic clue, because most fuse boxes have a legend printed on the inside of the cover or in the owner's manual that maps each numbered fuse slot to a specific circuit, so you can often guess which fuse to check before you even pop the cover.

It is worth noting that a fuse blowing once, especially after you plugged in a new phone charger or dash cam, is not necessarily a red flag. But if the same fuse keeps blowing repeatedly after you replace it, that usually means there is an underlying short or overloaded circuit, and simply replacing the fuse again and again is a band-aid, not a fix.

What You'll Need: Tools, Cost, and Time

This is one of the most beginner-friendly repairs in the DIY world, and the tool list is short. Here is what to gather before you start:

  • Your owner's manual, or a phone with the model-specific video pulled up from CarCareKiosk
  • A fuse puller tool (often built into the fuse box lid already, or included in an assortment kit)
  • A fuse assortment kit covering common automotive amp ratings (5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30 amp are the usual suspects)
  • A small flashlight or your phone's flashlight, since fuse boxes are often in dim spots
  • Optional but genuinely useful: a simple automotive test light or a multimeter set to continuity or DC voltage mode

Rough estimates for this job: difficulty is beginner level, time investment is 10 to 20 minutes for a first attempt, and total cost is typically $8 to $15 for a fuse assortment kit that will last you years, plus another $10 to $20 if you want to add a basic multimeter, which is a smart general-purpose tool to own anyway. Compare that to a shop visit, where many places charge a flat diagnostic or shop minimum fee of $50 to $100 just to look at the car, even before parts, for a problem you may be able to solve yourself in the time it takes to watch one short video.

Step-by-Step: Finding and Testing the Fuse

Most vehicles actually have two fuse boxes: one under the dashboard, usually to the left of the steering column or behind a small panel, and one under the hood, near the battery, which is often called the power distribution box. The under-hood box typically handles higher-draw items like the headlights, engine cooling fan, and starter relay, while the interior box handles things like the radio, power windows, and interior lights. Follow these steps to track down and confirm the culprit.

  1. Identify the symptom precisely. Note exactly what stopped working, since this narrows down which fuse to check first.
  2. Locate both fuse boxes using your owner's manual or a quick search on CarCareKiosk for your exact year, make, and model.
  3. Open the fuse box cover and find the printed diagram, usually on the underside of the cover itself, that lists each fuse number and the circuit it controls.
  4. Use the fuse puller tool to gently pull the suspect fuse straight out. Avoid using metal tools like pliers, which can bend the fuse's prongs or slip and short something.
  5. Hold the fuse up to the light and look at the thin metal strip inside. If it is visibly melted, broken, or blackened, that confirms your diagnosis visually.
  6. If the fuse looks fine visually but you are not fully sure, test it with a multimeter set to continuity mode by touching one probe to each prong. A steady beep or a reading near zero ohms means it is good; no beep means it is blown.
  7. Select a replacement fuse with the exact same amp rating, which is printed on the top of the fuse and color-coded for quick reference.
  8. Press the new fuse firmly into the same slot until it seats flush with the others around it.
  9. Test the affected feature immediately, whether that is the turn signal, radio, or power outlet, to confirm the fix worked.
  10. If the new fuse blows again right away or within a few days, stop replacing it and treat this as a sign of a deeper electrical issue rather than repeating the swap.
Safety note: Always match the exact amp rating printed on the original fuse. Installing a higher-rated fuse than the circuit calls for removes the built-in protection and risks melting wiring or starting an electrical fire. Never bridge a fuse slot with foil, wire, or any improvised substitute, even temporarily.

DIY Versus Shop Cost Comparison

The financial case for doing this yourself is about as clear-cut as car maintenance gets. A shop diagnosing an electrical gremlin will often start the clock the moment a technician opens the hood, and diagnostic time is billed at the shop's hourly labor rate, commonly $100 to $180 per hour depending on your area. Even a fast, straightforward fuse diagnosis can land on a $60 to $120 invoice once you add the shop's diagnostic fee and the marked-up cost of the fuse itself, which might retail for under a dollar.

At home, a $10 fuse assortment kit likely already has the exact amp rating you need, and it will cover you for years of future fuse swaps across multiple vehicles in your household. This is the kind of small, repeatable savings that adds up over time, similar in spirit to the ideas covered in what car maintenance actually costs, where skipping shop markups on simple jobs quietly protects your budget. Building this kind of basic skill also fits naturally into a broader plan like the one outlined in how to build a car maintenance schedule, so small issues get caught and handled before they snowball.

When to See a Mechanic Instead

Replacing a single blown fuse is a great first repair, but there are situations where you should stop and bring in a professional. If a fuse blows again immediately after replacement, if you notice a burning smell, visible melted plastic, or scorched wiring near the fuse box, or if multiple unrelated systems fail at once, these are signs of a wiring short or a failing component that needs proper diagnostic equipment to trace safely. Chasing an intermittent short yourself with only a fuse kit can waste hours and, in rare cases, create a fire risk if you keep forcing power through a damaged circuit.

It is also worth checking your car's other basic systems while you have the hood up, since electrical gremlins sometimes trace back to a weak battery connection rather than the fuse box itself. A quick look at how to check your car's fluids and a glance at your battery terminals for corrosion can rule out a couple of easy suspects before you assume the wiring itself is at fault. Quick Recap

  1. Note exactly which feature stopped working to narrow down the likely fuse.
  2. Find both fuse boxes, one inside the cabin and one under the hood.
  3. Read the fuse diagram printed on the cover to identify the right fuse number.
  4. Pull the fuse with a fuse puller tool, not metal pliers.
  5. Check the metal strip visually or test it with a multimeter for continuity.
  6. Buy a replacement with the exact same amp rating as the original.
  7. Press the new fuse in firmly until it sits flush.
  8. Test the feature right away to confirm the repair worked.
  9. If it blows again quickly, stop and treat it as a wiring issue, not a repeat fuse job.
  10. See a mechanic for repeat blows, burning smells, or multiple failed systems at once.

FAQ

Common questions

How do I know which fuse controls the part that stopped working?

Check the diagram printed on the inside of your fuse box cover or in your owner's manual, which lists each numbered fuse slot next to the circuit it controls, such as radio, turn signals, or power outlets.

Can I use a higher amp fuse if I don't have the right size on hand?

No. Always replace a fuse with the exact same amp rating as the original. A higher rated fuse removes the circuit's protection and can lead to overheating, melted wiring, or a fire.

Is it safe to check fuses myself, or should I always go to a shop?

Checking and replacing a fuse is one of the safest first DIY car repairs, since you are working with low-voltage circuits and a simple pull-and-swap tool. Just avoid metal tools that could short something else, and stop and consult a mechanic if a new fuse blows again right away.

How often should I check my car's fuses?

There is no set schedule for healthy fuses since they only need attention when something electrical stops working. It is more useful to know where both fuse boxes are located and keep a small assortment kit in your glovebox so you are ready whenever an issue pops up.
